# Background: the lunar south pole and Artemis

Why the Moon's south pole is where NASA wants to land, what makes it hard,
and how the nine regions in the app were chosen.

## Why the light is so strange

The Moon's spin axis is tilted only about 1.5° from upright relative to the
Sun. At the poles the Sun never climbs high: it circles near the horizon
once a month, rising or sinking a little with the seasons. So:

- **High ground can stay lit for most of the year.** Ridges and peaks catch
  the low Sun for long stretches. They are the best places for solar power,
  which is why the nine regions sit on rims, ridges, massifs and plateaus.
- **Low ground can stay dark forever.** Some crater floors never see the
  Sun. These permanently shadowed regions are among the coldest places
  measured in the solar system.
- **Shadows are long and move all the time.** A hill a few kilometers away,
  or a mountain 100 km off, can block the Sun. That's why the app uses real
  terrain for every site.
- **Nights are set by terrain, not by a fixed day.** Elsewhere on the Moon,
  day and night each last about two weeks. Near the pole a night can be a
  few days or more than two weeks, depending on what's in the way.

## Why Earth is hard to see

The Moon keeps the same face toward Earth, but from the south pole Earth
sits low on the horizon, near the edge of that face. The Moon's slight
wobble (libration) makes Earth rise and sink a few degrees over each month,
so crater rims and mountains hide it for days at a time. Direct radio
contact needs Earth in view, which is why the app also models relay
satellites.

## Why go there anyway

- **Sunlight for power** on the high ground, more of the time than anywhere
  else on the Moon.
- **Water ice** in the cold shadowed craters nearby. Ice could one day give
  drinking water, oxygen and rocket fuel. In 2009 NASA's LCROSS mission
  struck the shadowed floor of Cabeus crater and found water in the plume
  it threw up. Later studies of orbital data found ice exposed at the
  surface in some shadowed areas.
- **Science:** the cold traps may hold a long record of water and other
  frozen material delivered to the Moon, and the region has some of the
  Moon's oldest terrain.

The app shows how far each region is from the nearest shadowed area, for
information only. It never counts ice in the kit or the ranking.

## What makes it hard

- **Rugged ground:** steep slopes, boulders and craters. Two landers near
  the pole tipped over: IM-1 (2024) on a slope of about 12°, and IM-2 (2025)
  in a small crater.
- **Low Sun:** long shadows make the ground hard to read for landing and for
  crews on foot.
- **Cold:** shadowed ground stays far below freezing; equipment has to be
  kept warm.
- **Communications:** Earth drops behind the terrain for days.

## Artemis

Artemis is NASA's program to return astronauts to the Moon, with
international and commercial partners.

- **Artemis I (2022):** an uncrewed Orion spacecraft flew around the Moon
  and back.
- **Artemis II (2026):** four astronauts flew around the Moon in Orion.
- **Artemis III:** the planned first landing, near the south pole. NASA's
  dates for it have moved more than once, and the app doesn't track them.

## How the nine regions were chosen

In 2022 NASA named 13 candidate landing regions near the south pole. In
October 2024 it updated the list to nine: Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons
Mouton, Mons Mouton Plateau, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, Peak Near Cabeus B,
Slater Plain and de Gerlache Rim 2. The US Geological Survey published their
outlines.

NASA weighed things like:

- **Launch windows:** being able to reach the site on a range of dates.
- **Terrain:** slopes gentle enough to land and work on.
- **Lighting:** sunlight across the planned surface stay.
- **Communications:** direct contact with Earth.
- **Science:** varied geology, and being near permanently shadowed areas.

## How the navigator fits in

The app takes NASA's nine regions as given. It doesn't judge NASA's
choice. It asks a different, narrower question for its own scenario: a
crew of 4 staying a year, powered by solar panels and batteries. Where in
each region should they land, and what power kit would they have to bring?
It ranks spots inside each region by that kit weight. Its mission is a
planning scenario, not NASA's Artemis III plan: it says nothing about
NASA's actual schedule, rockets, landers or crew.
